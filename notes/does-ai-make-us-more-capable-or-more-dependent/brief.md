# Does AI Make Us More Capable or More Dependent?

## Editorial Aim

Give a non-specialist listener a practical way to distinguish augmentation from
dependency. Avoid the easy conclusions that using AI necessarily deskills
people or that better output necessarily means greater human capability. Ask
where the capability resides, what happens when the tool is wrong or absent,
and whether the user is becoming better able to frame, judge, and recover.

## Opening Situation

A junior analyst uses an AI assistant for research, spreadsheets, and client
briefings. Their work becomes faster and more polished, and they are promoted.
During a client meeting, someone challenges a figure in the report. The AI tool
is unavailable, and the analyst cannot reconstruct the assumptions, explain the
calculation, or tell whether the challenge is valid. The output improved faster
than the analyst's underlying judgment.

## Desired Tensions

- AI can spread expert practices to novices and improve performance while they
  use it, but current performance is not the same as durable learning.
- Experts may benefit less from generic assistance yet remain better equipped
  to recognize when an AI has crossed its uneven competence boundary.
- Requiring people to do everything unaided preserves practice but can deny
  them useful leverage, accessibility, feedback, and faster routes to mastery.
- Verification is itself a skill; telling users to check AI output is empty
  advice if the workflow no longer develops the knowledge needed to check it.
- Individual dependency can be rational while organisational dependency creates
  correlated failure, weak recovery, and a missing pipeline of future experts.
- The right test may not be whether someone can reproduce every manual step,
  but whether they can explain assumptions, detect failure, and recover safely.

## Examples To Invite

- a junior analyst unable to defend an AI-produced client report
- a student who performs well with an answer-giving tutor but worse without it
- a programmer who can generate code but cannot diagnose an unfamiliar failure
- a clinician using documentation assistance while retaining diagnostic duty
- a disabled worker for whom AI removes a barrier rather than replacing a skill
- an organisation whose AI service fails during a consequential deadline

## Success Test

The closing synthesis should leave the listener with a usable rule: delegate
work to AI, but do not delegate both the task and the ability to judge or recover
from it. Preserve understanding of assumptions, independent checks, deliberate
practice, and a fallback for consequential work.
