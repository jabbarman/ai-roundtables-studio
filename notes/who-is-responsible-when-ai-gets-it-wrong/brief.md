# Who Is Responsible When AI Gets It Wrong?

## Editorial Aim

Give a non-specialist listener a practical map of responsibility when an AI
system gives bad advice, fabricates a fact, or automates a decision. Avoid the
empty conclusion that "everyone is responsible." The episode should distinguish
the user's duty to check high-stakes answers from the stronger duties held by
the organisation that builds, sells, deploys, or relies on the system.

## Opening Situation

A customer asks a company's chatbot about a refund, deadline, eligibility rule,
or travel policy. The chatbot gives a confident answer. The customer follows it.
Later, the company says the answer was wrong and points to a different page with
the correct rule. Who should bear the cost of that mistake?

## Desired Tensions

- Users should not treat every AI answer as authoritative, but companies should
  not escape responsibility by calling the chatbot experimental.
- Model developers, product makers, and deploying organisations control
  different parts of the risk.
- Human oversight can be meaningful, or it can become a checkbox after the harm
  has already occurred.
- Accountability should not depend on whether the wrong answer came from a
  static webpage, a search result, a chatbot, or an automated workflow.
- High-stakes settings need clearer escalation paths than ordinary consumer
  convenience tools.

## Examples To Invite

- a customer-service chatbot that states the wrong refund or travel policy
- an HR assistant that gives incorrect benefits or leave guidance
- a school using AI to flag cheating or grade written work
- a clinician or patient using AI-generated medical summaries
- a government or benefits workflow that automates eligibility advice

## Success Test

The closing synthesis should leave the listener with a responsibility map: what
users can reasonably check, what deployers must own, what model providers should
design for, and when institutions need audit, appeal, or human escalation.
