# Who Should Control Powerful AI?

## Editorial Aim

Close Series One by moving from individual choices to institutional authority.
Give a non-specialist listener a practical map of who should decide what:
companies, evaluators, standards bodies, elected governments, courts, users,
markets, and international institutions. Avoid treating "control" as a single
lever or assuming that technical expertise automatically confers democratic
legitimacy.

## Opening Situation

An AI company is preparing to release a substantially more capable model. It
can complete long sequences of online work and is unusually effective at
finding software vulnerabilities. Internal testing finds valuable defensive
uses, but also signs that safeguards can be bypassed.

A rival is close to releasing a competing model. The government depends on the
company voluntarily providing access for its own tests. Independent researchers
cannot inspect the system fully, and businesses are already planning to deploy
it. The company argues that a delay could withhold benefits and hand the market
to a less responsible rival.

Who gets to decide whether release proceeds? What evidence must they see? Who
can impose conditions or stop deployment, and who holds that controller
accountable?

## Desired Tensions

- Laboratories have the deepest operational knowledge but also financial and
  competitive conflicts of interest.
- Governments can exercise legitimate coercive power, but may lack technical
  capacity, move too slowly, misuse access, or be captured by incumbents.
- Independent evaluators can improve the evidence base without possessing a
  democratic mandate to choose society's acceptable level of risk.
- Markets and users reveal demand and local context, but many harms are imposed
  on people who never chose the system.
- Rules aimed at the largest closed models may entrench incumbents or become
  impossible to apply to open and internationally distributed development.
- International scientific coordination can create shared evidence, but
  enforcement and political legitimacy remain largely national.
- Emergency powers may be necessary for serious threats, but need thresholds,
  time limits, reasons, review, and appeal.

## Examples To Invite

- a regulator delaying a model release after contested cyber testing
- an authoritarian state using safety oversight to demand broad model access
- a small open-model developer facing compliance costs an incumbent can absorb
- a hospital or public agency deploying a generally available model in a
  high-stakes setting
- incompatible rules causing a company to release in one country but not
  another
- an independent evaluator raising a false alarm that delays a useful system

## Success Test

The closing synthesis should reject both "trust the lab" and "let government
control everything." It should leave the listener with a usable allocation of
authority: the greater the plausible public harm and the less reversible it is,
the less release should depend on the developer alone. Developers must produce
evidence; qualified independent bodies must be able to test it; democratically
accountable authorities must set and enforce the thresholds; affected people
need notice and routes to challenge decisions; and emergency restrictions must
be reviewable and time-limited.
