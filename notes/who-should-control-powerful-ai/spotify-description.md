# Spotify Description Draft

Who should decide whether a powerful AI system is safe enough to release?

This Series One finale begins with an AI company preparing to launch a much more
capable model. It can carry out long sequences of online work and find software
vulnerabilities that defenders could use. But internal tests show that its
safeguards can be bypassed. A rival is close to release, government testing
depends on voluntary access, and independent researchers cannot inspect the
system fully.

The roundtable asks who may delay the release: the company, a government agency,
independent evaluators, a court, or no one. Can a national regulator make the
world safer, or does it merely disadvantage the cooperative lab while foreign
rivals release anyway? Could safety rules protect the public while also helping
incumbents suppress smaller or open-model competitors? And how can evaluators
inspect serious risks without handing governments unrestricted access to model
weights or sensitive technical details?

The central disagreement is whether a regulator should ever pause the base
model before real-world harm occurs. Google argues for targeted restrictions on
dangerous tools and deployments. OpenAI and Anthropic would permit a temporary
model-level pause when strong capability, realistic safeguard bypass, and
inadequate controls appear together.

The strongest rule that survives is risk-tiered authority: laboratories provide
evidence, qualified independent evaluators test it, democratically accountable
governments impose proportionate conditions or pauses, courts review decisions
quickly, and restrictions expire unless renewed with fresh evidence.

The episode is informed by the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI obligations, the
NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the UK AI Security Institute's frontier
testing, the OECD AI Principles, and the UN Independent International Scientific
Panel on AI.
